Inside the specification

What the document actually contains.

Not a slide deck and not a sales proposal. A working document, written in plain English, structured like this:

  1. What we're buildingThe product argued down to what version one actually needs — with the reasoning shown, so you can push back.
  2. The technical approachThe tools, the hosting, and what it costs to run each month, in plain English.
  3. How it reaches usersThe App Store or the web — and any compliance requirements that come with your field: HIPAA, payments, data privacy.
  4. The build planMilestones in a sensible order, with what each one puts in your hands.
  5. The priceA fixed number for the build — not an estimate that grows.
  6. What waitsEverything cut from the first version, set aside where it can't inflate the budget.

Before you commit

Questions people ask before spending $5,000.

What if the answer is “don't build”?

Then that's what the specification says, and you've saved many times the fee. Plenty of problems that look like software problems aren't — often the fix is a process change, a renegotiated contract, or configuring something you already pay for. I'd rather tell you that in week two than start a build I don't believe in. A build only happens if the specification says “build” — and the document is yours either way.

What exactly do I get for $5,000?

A written specification you own: the product argued down to what version one actually needs, the technical approach in plain English, how it will reach users, any compliance requirements, a milestone schedule, and a fixed price for the build — a real number, not a range. You can take it to any builder, including one who isn't me. The fee is fixed, agreed before we start, and isn't credited against the build — the specification is worth having whether or not I'm the one who builds from it.

Can we skip Definition and go straight to a build?

No — Definition is the only way in. A build can't be priced honestly before the definition work is done; skipping it means bidding on a guess, and a fixed bid built on a guess punishes us both. If you already have notes, sketches, or a document from another developer, bring them — good input makes Definition faster and sharper, not unnecessary.

How is this different from hiring an agency?

An agency puts a salesperson on discovery, a project manager in the middle, and junior developers on the actual building — the product that ships is three translations away from what you described. I write the code myself, so there's no handoff to hide. The trade is honest: I'm one person, I can't throw five engineers at a deadline, and I'll tell you when a project is bigger than I should take on. What you get instead is the same brain from decision to App Store — and the same person telling you when a feature isn't worth building.

Will I own what you produce?

Yes — all of it. Code, designs, documents, and the intellectual property, plus documentation so you're never locked into working only with me. I don't resell software or take vendor commissions either, so nothing I hand you is quietly steering you toward a product that pays me.

What's not included?

Two things clients often assume are included, and aren't: ongoing feature development after launch — that's a new engagement, or new work under Support billed at $200/hour — and your marketing site. A few things stay your responsibility rather than mine: the App Store developer account, the business entity, the terms of service, and any regulatory or clinical sign-off your product needs. I'll help you navigate all of them, but they're yours, and the agreement says so plainly.

How do payments work?

Definition is a fixed $5,000, agreed up front. Builds are a fixed bid priced from the specification — first builds start at $35,000, and most real products land between $45,000 and $60,000 — paid 35% to start, 30% mid-build, and 35% on completion, against milestones you can actually see. Anything added mid-build is a change order at my hourly rate, agreed in writing before the work happens. Support is a monthly retainer between $2,500 and $4,000, month to month — stop whenever it stops being worth it.

Can I talk to a reference first?

Yes, and I'd encourage it. I'll put you in touch with clients who've been through this, including the ones named on this site. Some engagements are covered by confidentiality and can't be discussed publicly, but I'm glad to talk specifics privately.

Do you discount for nonprofits?

Often, yes — for nonprofits, educational institutions, and churches. A good share of my work is with them, and the pricing on this page isn't the end of the conversation. Tell me your situation on the call.
