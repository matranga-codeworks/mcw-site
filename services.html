Inside the assessment

What the document actually contains.

Not a slide deck and not a sales proposal. A working document, written in plain English, structured like this:

  1. Where you stand todayYour systems, what they cost you, and what's actually at risk — written so your board can follow it.
  2. What you're being soldA line-by-line read on any vendor quotes or proposals already on the table, and whether they're fair.
  3. Build or buyThe recommendation, with the reasoning shown so you can argue with it rather than take it on faith.
  4. What it will really costReal numbers and a sensible order of operations — what to do first, and what can wait.
  5. What happens if you do nothingThe honest version, including the cases where doing nothing is the right call.

Before you commit

Questions people ask before spending $6,000.

What if the answer is “don't build”?

Then that's what the assessment says, and you've saved many times the fee. Plenty of problems that look like software problems aren't — often the fix is a process change, a renegotiated contract, or configuring something you already pay for. I'd rather tell you that in week two than take on a build I don't believe in. The recommendation is the deliverable; a build only happens if the recommendation turns out to be “build.”

What exactly do I get for $6,000?

A written assessment of where you stand, in plain English, and a roadmap with real costs and a sensible order of operations. Alongside that: a read on any vendor quotes already on the table, and a build-or-buy recommendation with the reasoning shown so you can argue with it. It's a document you own and can take anywhere, including to a different consultant. The fee is fixed and agreed before we start — where it lands above $6,000 depends only on how much ground there is to cover.

How is this different from my IT company?

Your IT company keeps the computers on — networks, laptops, email, backups, the help desk. Judging whether a six-figure quote is fair, or whether a platform will still fit you in five years, is a different job. Most IT firms don't build software and don't price it, so they aren't equipped to evaluate it.

I'm not a replacement for them. I'm the person who reads the contract before you sign it, and who can oversee them on your behalf when the work gets technical.

Will I own what you produce?

Yes — all of it. Code, designs, documents, and the intellectual property, plus documentation so you're never locked into working only with me. I don't resell software or take vendor commissions either, so nothing I hand you is quietly steering you toward a product that pays me.

How do payments work?

The assessment is a fixed fee, agreed up front. Builds are a fixed bid paid against milestones you can actually see — typically around 25% to start, 50% across milestones, and 25% on completion — so you're never paying for work that hasn't been delivered, and never signing a blank check. Advisory is month to month, and you can stop whenever it stops being worth it.

Can I talk to a reference first?

Yes, and I'd encourage it. I'll put you in touch with clients who've been through this, including the ones named on this site. Some engagements are covered by confidentiality and can't be discussed publicly, but I'm glad to talk specifics privately.

Do you discount for nonprofits?

Often, yes — for nonprofits, educational institutions, and churches. A good share of my work is with them, and the pricing on this page isn't the end of the conversation. Tell me your situation on the call.
