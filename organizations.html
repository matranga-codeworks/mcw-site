Inside the specification

What the document contains.

Not a slide deck and not a sales pitch. A working document, in plain English:

  1. What we're buildingVersion one, argued down to what it needs, with the reasoning so you can push back.
  2. The technical approachThe tools, the hosting, and what it costs to run each month.
  3. What it connects toWhat moves over from the old system and spreadsheets, what it talks to (membership, accounting, donors, payments), and any rules your field follows, such as HIPAA or grant reporting.
  4. The build planWhat ships in month one, month two, and so on, in priority order.
  5. The budgetHow many months at $8,000, with the reasoning, and what gets cut first if the estimate slips.
  6. RolloutHow staff get trained and how the switch from the old system happens.
  7. What waitsEverything cut from version one, set aside where it can't inflate the budget.

Before you commit

Questions people ask before spending $3,000.

What if the answer is “don't build”?

Then that's what the specification says, and you've saved many times the fee. Plenty of problems that look like software problems aren't. Often the fix is a process change, a renegotiated contract, or setting up something you already pay for. I'd rather tell you that in week one than start a build I don't believe in. The document is yours either way.

What exactly do I get for $3,000?

A written specification you own: version one argued down to what it needs, the technical approach in plain English, what moves over from your current system and what it connects to, any rules you have to follow, a month-by-month plan, and an estimate of how many build months it takes. The budget is $8,000 times a number you've seen the reasoning for. You can take it to any builder. The fee is fixed and isn't credited against the build.

Can we skip Discovery and go straight to a build?

Discovery comes first for every engagement. A build can't be estimated before the discovery work is done, and a month count built on a guess punishes us both. If you already have notes, board minutes, or a document from another developer, bring them. Good input makes Discovery faster, not unnecessary.

Why a monthly price instead of a fixed one?

A fixed price only works when the requirements hold still, and they rarely do. Priorities shift the moment staff see working software, and a fixed-price contract turns each shift into a change order and a negotiation. The retainer fixes the price and the pace and leaves the contents of each month to you. For a budget, it's a predictable line item instead of one large capital request, and you can stop at any month boundary holding working software.

What if the build runs past the estimate?

You'll see it coming. Every monthly summary restates months shipped and months remaining, so a slip shows up in writing the month it starts. When it does, the choice is yours and the price isn't: cut what's left to hold the budget, or add a month at the same $8,000. The monthly price can't rise, and you can stop at any month boundary with working software you own. Most first versions land in three to six months.

We need a fixed total for a grant or an RFP. Can you do that?

Yes, when the fixed total is imposed from outside: a grant deliverable, an RFP, a regulator, or a partner's specification. Then I quote a fixed fee against the Discovery specification, from $20,000, paid in three stages: 35% to start, 30% mid-build, and 35% on completion. Anything outside the specification is priced as a change order before the work happens. It's the exception, because a fixed price has to be padded against unknowns a monthly arrangement absorbs.

How do payments work?

Discovery is a fixed $3,000, agreed up front. The build is $8,000 a month, invoiced at the start of each month. The first three months are a commitment on both sides, so the smallest total is $27,000. After that it's month to month with 30 days' notice either way. No change orders: anything added goes on a later month's list. Once the product is live, maintenance is $2,500 a month for up to ten hours or $4,000 for up to twenty, month to month.

How is this different from hiring an agency?

With an agency, one person sells the project, another manages it, and a third builds it, and what ships has been translated twice. With me, you explain it once, to the person who builds it. The trade-off: I'm one person, I can't put five engineers on a deadline, and I'll say so when a project is bigger than I should take on. In exchange you get software that matches what you described, and someone who'll tell you when a feature isn't worth the money.

What happens if you're unavailable?

Less than you'd fear. Everything is documented as it's built, and the code, designs, and accounts are in your name, so another developer can pick it up from the documentation. For overflow or a gap I have contractors I trust, brought in only with your knowledge and with me still responsible. I carry general and professional liability insurance, certificates on request, and will sign a business associate agreement where your data requires one.

Will I own what you produce?

Yes, all of it. Code, designs, documents, and the rights, plus documentation so you're never locked in to me. I don't resell software or take vendor commissions, so nothing I hand you steers you toward a product that pays me.

What's not included?

Two things people assume are included and aren't: new features after launch, which are a build month or as-needed work at $200 an hour, and your marketing site. A few things stay yours: the hosting, payment, and vendor accounts, opened in your name; the decision about which records move over from the old system; and any legal or regulatory sign-off. I'll help with all of them, but the agreement says plainly that they're yours.

Can I talk to a reference first?

Yes, and I'd encourage it. I'll put you in touch with clients who've been through this, including the ones named on this site. Some engagements are confidential and can't be discussed publicly, but I'm glad to talk specifics privately.

Do you discount for nonprofits?

The price doesn't move, but the scope does. A good share of my work is with nonprofits, and the way to meet a smaller budget is a smaller first version or fewer months, not a cheaper month. I'll show you what comes out and what it costs to leave it out. Every client pays the price on this page, which is what makes it a number you can carry to a board.
