How I got here

A career spent building, at every scale.

I learned the trade at scale. At OnePay, a venture-backed fintech, I was an engineer on a mobile banking app used by tens of millions of people — software that moves tens of billions of dollars a year. When the software you build is holding someone's money, you learn fast what “ready” really means, and what it costs to get there.

Then I led the engineering team at Huml Health, a healthtech startup building wearable devices for patients going through substance-abuse detox and recovery. Going from one engineer among many at a billion-dollar company to the lead at a tiny startup taught me the other half of the job: making the call, owning the roadmap, and answering for the outcome when there's no one above you to escalate to.

Alongside all of that, I spent five years building software for Preston Center of Compassion, a nonprofit in the Bronx. That's what showed me what I actually wanted to do. Working with a local, nontechnical organization, you see exactly who your work helps and how much it matters — the impact is right in front of you in a way it never is when you're one engineer among hundreds at a company moving billions.

What I kept noticing was that the people who most needed software built — founders without an engineer, organizations without technical leadership — were the least likely to have anyone who could build it well, or tell them what not to build. They didn't need a big agency, and they didn't need an order-taker. They needed one person who could make the product decisions, design the architecture, write the code, and ship it — and push back along the way. So that's what I do now, and nothing gets lost in a handoff, because there isn't one.

What I believe about software decisions

01

Sometimes the answer is to build nothing.

Plenty of problems that look like software problems aren't. Often the fix is changing a process, renegotiating with a vendor, or simply waiting — and saying so out loud is part of what you're paying me for.

02

Advice is only as good as the hands behind it.

I can tell you what something will really cost because I quoted and shipped that exact work this year — not because I read a report about it. That's why I never stopped building.

Proof, if you want it: I keep a technical blog at matranga.dev ↗ — mostly for other developers.

03

You should own the plan, not rent it.

Whatever I hand you is written in plain language, tied to no vendor, and yours to take anywhere — including to someone other than me.
