How I got here

A career spent building, now spent advising.

Before I advised anyone, I built at scale. At OnePay, a venture-backed fintech, I was a full-stack engineer on a mobile banking app used by tens of millions of people — software that moves tens of billions of dollars a year. When the code you ship is holding someone's money, you learn fast what “production-ready” really means, and what it costs to get there.

Then I led the engineering team at Huml Health, a healthtech startup building wearable devices for patients going through substance-abuse detox and recovery. Going from one engineer among many at a unicorn to the lead at a tiny startup taught me the other half of the job: making the call, owning the roadmap, and answering for the outcome when there's no one above you to escalate to.

Alongside all of that, I spent five years building software for Preston Center of Compassion, a nonprofit in the Bronx. That's what showed me what I actually wanted to do. Working with a local, nontechnical organization, you see exactly who your work helps and how much it matters — the impact is right in front of you in a way it never is when you're one engineer among hundreds at a company moving billions.

What I kept noticing was that the organizations who needed technical judgment most — nonprofits, associations, midsize companies — were the least likely to have anyone inside who could give it. They didn't need another vendor. They needed someone in their corner who could read a quote, weigh a decision, and still write the code when building was the right answer. So that's what I do now, and I never put the keyboard down — the advice is only worth as much as the hands behind it.

What I believe about software decisions

01

Sometimes the answer is to build nothing.

Plenty of problems that look like software problems aren't. Often the fix is changing a process, renegotiating with a vendor, or simply waiting — and saying so out loud is part of what you're paying me for.

02

Advice is only as good as the hands behind it.

I can tell you what something will really cost because I quoted and shipped that exact work this year — not because I read a report about it. That's why I never stopped building.

Proof, if you want it: I keep a technical blog at matranga.dev ↗ — mostly for other developers.

03

You should own the plan, not rent it.

Whatever I hand you is written in plain language, tied to no vendor, and yours to take anywhere — including to someone other than me.
