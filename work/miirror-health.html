Case study · Startup · Digital health

A clinically-governed mental health product, shipped to the App Store.

Miirror Health needed a real product for eating-disorder recovery, where the wrong content in front of the wrong patient is a clinical risk — not a UX complaint. There was nothing usable to build on. I built the whole thing.

The situation What made it hard What I built Where it landed

The client and the situation

Miirror Health is an early-stage digital health company building a platform for eating-disorder recovery — specifically the part of care that happens between appointments, when a patient is alone with an app instead of in a room with a clinician.

When the founder came to me there was no working product. There was an inherited prototype, built by a student team, that had been engineered into something nobody remaining could maintain, and that was already costing hundreds of dollars a month to run before it had a single user. The first question wasn't what to build. It was whether any of what existed was worth keeping.

My answer was no, and that was the whole first engagement: a written recommendation to start over with something deliberately simpler and cheaper to operate. It cost me the more comfortable, open-ended project of nursing the existing system along, which is generally how you can tell the recommendation was honest.

What made it hard

Three things, and none of them were about the technology.

The user population is vulnerable. In eating-disorder recovery, content is not neutral. Material that supports one patient's recovery can actively harm another's, and the difference is not something a product manager can eyeball. That meant per-user control over what each patient can see — and it meant those controls had to be operated by clinicians, in language clinicians use, not buried in an engineering console.

The safety rules weren't mine to write. Miirror Health's clinical advisory board defined and approved the feature set, including the rules governing which content is appropriate for which patient. I built to that specification. Keeping that boundary sharp matters for the patients first and for liability second: clinical judgment belongs to clinicians, and a developer improvising it — however well-intentioned — is exactly the failure mode this product could not have.

It was regulated health data from day one. HIPAA is cheap to design in and ruinous to bolt on afterward. Starting over turned out to be the one advantage here — there was no existing product to retrofit, so compliance shaped the architecture instead of fighting it.

What I built

The whole product: the app patients use; the system behind it that holds the clinical content, the per-patient safety rules, and the records; a dashboard where clinicians manage their own patients and control what each one sees; and the admin tools for the team behind it.

Plus the parts nobody demos — multi-factor authentication, audit logging, the data handling HIPAA requires, and App Store submission, which is its own project when the app is a health product being reviewed as one.

Every one of those decisions was made by the person who then had to implement it. No scoping document handed down to a developer, no estimate written by someone who wouldn't have to meet it.

Only now is the stack worth mentioning: Django on PostgreSQL, with React Native on the front end. Standard, boring, thoroughly documented tools — chosen so the engineer they eventually hire recognizes the codebase on day one, and so the monthly bill stays proportional to a company at their stage. That was the failure of the thing they'd inherited, and it wasn't going to be the failure of this one.

Where it landed

Shipped to the App Store for beta, with the clinician dashboard and admin interface live alongside it — a working product where there had been a prototype nobody could safely run.

I still answer for it, and I'm the one who picks up the phone now that it's in patients' hands.
