Who this is for

Three ways founders end up on this page.

Nothing exists yet

You've validated the idea the honest way — conversations, a waitlist, a spreadsheet someone is actually using. Now something real has to exist, and there's no one to build it.

You inherited a mess

An agency, a contractor, or a team of students built you something. It half works, it costs more every month than it should, and nobody left can explain how it fits together.

You're about to hire

Your first engineer, or a dev shop with a convincing deck. Either way you're making a technical judgment call with no technical person on your side of the table.

What these have in common: something real has to get built, it has to be built right the first time, and there's no one on your side of the table who builds. That's the whole job.

Where they were

An early-stage startup building a digital platform for eating-disorder recovery — clinical care with real safety obligations, not a normal app. The founder had inherited a platform built by students: over-engineered, unmaintainable, and already costing hundreds of dollars a month before it had even launched. They needed an honest read on whether it was worth saving.

What I said

It wasn't. The product carried far more complexity and cost than an early-stage company should, with no realistic path to maintain it. Shipped — through App Store review and into patients' hands. I built the whole product: a Django backend on PostgreSQL, a React Native patient app, the clinician dashboard, and the admin interface, with the safety work the domain demands — per-user controls over what content each patient can safely see, multi-factor authentication, and audit logging.

Before you commit

Questions founders ask first.

How small should the first version be?

Smaller than you think, and it's the first thing we'll argue about. Most first versions carry three times the features they need, which triples the cost and delays the only thing that matters — putting something real in front of real users. My job early on is to find the shortest path to a product you can learn from, and build that. The rest waits until someone outside your organization has told you it's missing.

I inherited a codebase. Can you tell me whether it's worth saving?

Yes, and it's a common way founders start with me. That's a Definition engagement: a fixed-fee, two-week phase that ends in a straight answer — keep it, fix it, or start over — with real numbers attached to each. Sometimes what you inherited is fine and just needs an owner. Sometimes it carries far more complexity and cost than an early-stage company should, and the honest recommendation is to rebuild something much simpler. I've given both answers.

Do you take equity instead of cash?

No. I work for a fee, and that's deliberate — it means I have no stake in your product growing bigger than it needs to be. If a build is out of reach at your stage, say so on the call. I'd rather scope it down to something you can actually afford than take a position in your company.

Who owns the code?

You do — all of it. Code, designs, documents, accounts, and the intellectual property, plus documentation so you're never locked into working only with me. This matters more for you than for most of my clients: an investor doing diligence will ask, and “our contractor owns it” is not an answer you want to give.

What's not included?

Two things founders often assume are included, and aren't: ongoing feature development after launch — that's a new engagement, or new work under Support billed at $200/hour — and your marketing site. A few things stay your responsibility rather than mine: the App Store developer account, the business entity, the terms of service, and any regulatory or clinical sign-off your product needs. I'll help you navigate all of them, but they're yours, and the agreement says so plainly.

What happens when we hire our own engineers?

That's the goal, and I build for it from the start — no clever tricks, documented as I go, built with standard tools a new engineer will recognize on day one. When you're ready to bring the work in-house I hand it over and help your first engineer find their footing. I'm glad to sit in on those interviews too; judging a technical hire is not something a non-technical founder should have to do alone.

How is this different from hiring an agency?

An agency puts a salesperson on discovery, a project manager in the middle, and junior developers on the implementation — the product that ships is three translations away from what you described. I write the code myself, so there's no handoff to hide. The trade is honest: I'm one person, I can't throw five engineers at a deadline, and I'll tell you when a project is bigger than I should take on. What you get instead is the same brain from decision to App Store — and the same person telling you when a feature isn't worth building.
